Executive Summary

Net income nearly doubled despite a slight revenue decline

Net income reached $610,096 for the TTM period, up 88.2% year over year, even though total revenue fell 2.3% to $25.5M. Milk produced rose 4.5% to 361,247 cwt on a herd that grew 7.5% to 1,085 head.

Margins improved sharply despite the revenue dip: gross margin rose from 70.0% to 83.0% and operating margin from 1.6% to 3.0%, driven almost entirely by a 44.5% drop in COGS. That drop is concentrated in one place — COGS fell from roughly $600–750K/month to under $80K/month starting January 2026 — while total operating costs (COGS + expenses combined) fell only 3.6% over the same period. That pattern looks more like a change in how costs are being classified between COGS and expenses than an actual reduction in spending, and is worth confirming against the books before treating the margin gain as a genuine efficiency win.

Revenue remains heavily concentrated in one channel: milk products account for 92.7% of TTM revenue, with animal sales (6.7%) and custom work/other (0.8%) making up the rest. And with the milking herd now at capacity under current robotic milking infrastructure, further production growth will require capital investment in additional robots rather than herd expansion.

Strategic Finding

Rising prices, not just more head sold, are driving animal revenue growth

Animal revenue grew 58.7% to $1.34M while head sold grew only 16.7% (754 to 880), so most of the gain is price, not volume. Cull cows (Cows Over 2 Years) show the clearest trend: both head sold (46 to 75 per quarter, up 63%) and price ($1,471 to $1,887/head, up 28%) rose every quarter this year, tracking the national cull-cow market's run to record highs. Calves under 5 months, the largest category by volume (515 head), saw prices jump sharply in Jan-Mar '26 to $1,774/head before easing to $1,573, still up 42% from a year ago. Cows 5-24 MOS is the outlier: only 84 head all year, swinging from 5 to 43 head per quarter with no consistent price trend ($2,211 down to $1,593, then back up to $1,987), too small and erratic to read as a signal either way.

Even with that growth, animal sales remain a secondary business line, at roughly 6.7% of total company revenue. But it's growing meaningfully faster than the core milk business, and worth watching as a real, if modest, driver of this year's stronger bottom line.

Strategic Finding

Production and per-cow output are both ahead of target

Annual production is up 4.5% to 4.2M gallons, and per-cow output (96 lbs/day) is now running above the internal target of 94.3, about a 660 lbs/week pace per cow. That gain is coming from efficiency per cow, not herd growth: the milking herd has already topped out at current robot capacity.

The herd's genetic makeup has also shifted meaningfully toward A2 over the past year and a half, from 49% in Apr '25 to about 60% by Aug '26, with the untested/unknown share dropping from 16% to under 5% as more of the herd gets tested. Butterfat has stayed flat around 3.9% the whole time. Somatic cell count is running above the summer target (250K vs. the under-150K goal) and worth keeping an eye on, though it's not the main story here.

Weekly Production chart — methodology

Plots the weekly "Actual" readings from Casey's raw-milk KPI log directly, one point per week, with no monthly rollup — each of the two lines starts the week containing 7/1 (TTM Jul 2026 = weeks of 6/30/25–7/31/26; TTM Jul 2025 = weeks of 7/1/24–7/28/25) and runs through the latest available week, so each is ~13 months, not a strict trailing-12; the two are aligned week-for-week by position from that 7/1 anchor rather than by calendar date, since the weekly cadence isn't perfectly regular. The Annual Production stat card above is unaffected by this — it's still a strict trailing-twelve-month sum, so it won't exactly match a manual sum of the chart's longer window. That sheet's "Actual" column is labeled pounds but is actually gallons (confirmed against the KPI's own 2026 target note: 709,500 lbs ≈ 82,500 gal, and 82,500 is what's in the sheet); cwt is derived from it at 8.6 lbs/gal, matching the sheet's own cwt column. The y-axis does not start at zero — weekly production only moves in a ~16% band, so a zero baseline flattened it to near-invisible; the axis is zoomed to the data instead, with headroom below the minimum.

Measured against the county, our yields are in line with local norms

The Michigan state average overstates our gaps: against Oceana County, wheat (60 bu/a) runs 4.3% above the county's 57.5 bu/a average, and grain corn (~150 bu/a, a rough estimate) is within 2% of the county's 152.8 bu/a average, not the double-digit shortfalls the state comparison implied.
